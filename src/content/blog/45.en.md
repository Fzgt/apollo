## Evidence anchoring, against hallucination

`SourceDocument.evidence_for` checks that a quotation is an exact substring of the source, and rejects anything that is not. Models quoting a source routinely change a digit, merge two sentences, or paraphrase and still wrap it in quotation marks. Let that through and the verification stage is using text the model made up to verify a conclusion the model made up — which is no verification at all. This is the hardest design point I can talk an interviewer through.

The one-liner: "When the model quotes a source, we do not take its word that it is a quotation. We force it to give a substring that can be found character for character in the source, and if it cannot be found, it never reaches the next stage."

The follow-up — "why not just ask the model again whether it is sure?" The answer: a model will confirm its own invention just as confidently. Asking again only gets the same hallucination restated with more conviction; it is not verification. Substring matching is the only way to rule out "semantically similar but not actually the source".

## Blind review, against confirmation bias

The verification stage (Auditor) cannot see the research stage's (Explorer's) reasoning. It sees only the claim and the raw evidence. That is blind review — it stops the model from marking its own work as sound while verifying a conclusion it just invented.

The one-liner: "The verification stage sees only the conclusion and the raw evidence, never how the research stage arrived at it — otherwise it follows the same line of thought and ends up approving itself."

The follow-up — "why not let the model review itself?" The answer: reviewing itself in the same context, the model anchors on the reasoning chain it just produced and struggles to question it. An independent perspective that sees only claim plus evidence, and never the reasoning, cannot be led by the other side's phrasing.

## Failure as data, not as an exception: self-healing retries

The self-healing loop for structured output: when `LLMClient.structured` fails validation, the Pydantic error is fed straight back to the model so it can fix its own output, rather than being marked a failure. Self-repair within a retry budget costs less than a single perfect prompt.

The one-liner: "Retryable and non-retryable failures are separate types, and the caller has to handle them explicitly instead of guessing behind one `try`/`except`. When structured output fails validation, we feed the error text back to the model to fix, rather than failing the call outright."

The follow-up — "why not just fail and start again?" Because starting again spends a whole call rolling the dice a second time. Feeding the specific error back means the model is fixing something it now knows is wrong: higher success rate, lower cost.

## Budget orchestration

**1. The budget accumulates across replays; you cannot get around the limit by re-running.** Hermes can replay from a given stage without re-running the earlier stages that already passed validation, but `BudgetTracker` reseeds itself from the queries, fetches, and tokens recorded before this run. Replay three times and the budget counts all three, not a fresh allowance each time. That is the design that stops "fail and retry forever to cheat the budget" — plenty of agent systems leave that hole open.

**2. "The stage ran" and "the stage got it right" are separate judgements.** A stage adapter succeeding does not mean you may move on: the artifact still has to pass a gate assertion. Failing the gate is a logical failure and is never retried automatically — kept strictly apart from transient, retryable faults, with different labels in `StageRun.error` so the two never get mixed up afterwards. In other words, "the program did not error" and "this result is fit to use" are two independent criteria, and the first cannot stand in for the second.

**3. The budget is checked at exactly two boundaries, never in the middle of a call.** Once after a stage attempt finishes and once before the next begins — never during. So even if one call blows the budget, that call runs to completion; there simply is no next one. This is deliberate: better to overshoot slightly on the last call than to cut a model call off halfway.

The one-liner for all of it: "What most often goes out of control in an agent system is not the logic being wrong, it is that nobody is watching how many times it runs and how much it spends. Hermes makes that a hard boundary that accumulates across replays and never interrupts a call in flight." In a CV story, this shows an understanding of multi-agent engineering better than the four points above, because it answers the question almost every interviewer asks: how do you stop an agent burning money or looping forever?

# Python

## Structured concurrency, throttled at two levels

The one-liner: "When fetching many pages, a global semaphore caps total concurrency so we do not flatten ourselves or the other side, and requests to the same domain are queued and rate-limited separately. The two layers do different jobs. A single failed link does not throw; it comes back as a result object, so one failure does not blow up the whole batch."

The follow-up — "why is one global semaphore not enough?" Because a workable global concurrency number does not mean the traffic is evenly spread. In the worst case every concurrency slot points at the same domain at once, which is still a concentrated hammering from that server's point of view. So the per-domain lock and rate limit sit on top. Two layers dividing the work, not duplicating it.
